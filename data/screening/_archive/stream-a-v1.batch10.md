**DOI:** 10.1109/acssc.2016.7869063

**Keywords:** low-rank matrix; quantization; maximum likelihood; data privacy; Data privacy; Sparse matrices; Manganese; Phasor measurement units; Measurement uncertainty; Noise measurement; Maximum likelihood estimation

This paper recovers data from quantized and partially corrupted measurements. The data recovery is achieved through solving a constrained maximum likelihood estimation problem that exploits the low-rank property of the actual measurements. The recovery error is proven to be order optimal and decays in the same order as that of the state-of-the-art method when no corruption exists. The data accuracy is thus maintained while the data privacy is enhanced. A new application of this method for data privacy in power systems is discussed. Experiments on synthetic data and real synchrophasor data in power systems demonstrate the effectiveness of our method.

As far as we know, this was the first population-level and episode-based attempt at international analysis. This analysis was made possible with comprehensive population-level registers that allow record linkage at the individual level. The analyses of regional variation in stroke (Peltola et al., 2015) and hip fracture (Medin et al., 2015) are based on indicators calculated for about 100 regions in seven countries. The studies indicate that observed performance variations between regions and countries were not explained by either patient-level or regional-level factors. To understand the existing variations, more detailed data on the care of these patients, the setting, and the environment are required, and preferably, the variation should be analysed on a level lower than the regional level. A major constraint in the EuroHOPE project was—similarly to any study with individual-level administrative data—that not all countries allowed the export of individual-level datasets. Thus, the regional-level analyses were made using aggregate indicators produced from individual-level data from each country. In addition, for VLBW and VLGA infants (Fattore et al., 2015), a meta-analysis is performed in order to estimate model coefficients across countries. The study shows that socio-economic conditions, measured in terms of unemployment and per capita GDP, did matter in explaining different mortality rates across European regions, while concentration of care and the type of the delivery hospital had ambiguous effects on mortality when data for four countries were combined. The care of AMI is considered with both regional and individual-level data (Hagen et al., 2015a; Hagen et al., 2015b). The main focus of these articles is to analyse use of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and its effects on outcome. The first article explores the effect of healthcare system and regional characteristics on procedure intensity and mortality. The authors find that regions with for the most part activity-based reimbursement system had a 17% higher PCI rate than global budgeting systems, but this was not associated with lower mortality. The second article, based on individual-level data from Finland and Norway, examines whether the differences in one-year mortality between socioeconomic groups were related to the utilisation of PCI. Socio-economic differences in the use of PCI were only marginally associated to differences in mortality, and the authors conclude that in order to reduce the socio-economic inequalities in AMI mortality, health policy should focus on the living conditions and lifestyles of patients with low income and low education. Efficient hospital production implies a trade-off between quality and quantity or equivalently between quality and costs. If this holds true, the quality of a treatment cannot be increased without reducing the number of patients treated or alternatively using more resources. This cost–quality trade-off was analysed at hospital level from two angles. Häkkinen et al. (2015) examine the trade-off in three conditions (AMI, stroke, and hip fracture) after first studying the determinants of survival and costs separately in about 200 European hospitals. The cost–quality trade-off at aggregate hospital level is examined using data from four Nordic countries (Kittelsen et al., 2015). The studies did not find any clear cost–quality trade-off (except for the care of AMI in Hungary and Finland), implying that there was potential for improving hospital performance by containing cost or improving quality without increasing costs. Heijink et al. (2015) summarise the main results of all papers with respect to earlier literature. An important general conclusion is related to the fact that the large differences (national, regional, and hospital levels) observed in both the use of resources (length of stay) and health outcomes could not be easily explained by either demand or system level variables. This again indicates much room for improving healthcare performance and stresses the importance of international benchmarking of the best performing regions and hospitals. Our project indicates that using performance measurement based on individual-level data with standardised data collection is feasible and needs to be routinely carried out to improve international comparisons (The Lancet 2014) in order for this kind of benchmarking to have an impact on the performance of health systems. To sum up, the EuroHOPE project has contributed with new insights into international benchmarking by disentangling variations in performance at different levels of health systems and by developing procedures for data handling and assessing various methodological approaches. Similarly to EuroHOPE, future analyses will encounter challenges dealing with the practical difficulties of linking individual data across registers from several countries. A current OECD (2015) report illustrates that there are various alternatives to create a data governance framework for health information, including individual level data with linking possibilities that protects health information privacy. Use of linkable register data gives a great potential for improving the quality of international comparison and allows international benchmarking to be an important input in the process of European health policy-making. More generally, there is a need for creating a European-level research infrastructure, which allows researchers to compare the efficiency and performance of European healthcare systems, to analyse the reasons behind the observed differences, and to evaluate the effects on recent reforms on efficiency. It is widely recognised that the ability to follow patients as they progress through the healthcare system to death, including primary healthcare, hospital, and other specialised treatments, long-term care, home care and hospice care, is essential to healthcare quality and performance assessment. Thus, the approach developed in EuroHOPE should be extended to include patient pathways outside the hospital sector. Based on our findings, we propose future analyses to focus on extending our research on explaining the variation in utilization and outcomes from a population or provider perspective. More detailed analysis of differences between geographical areas could give more insight into how patient pathways can be improved in terms of outcomes and the use of resource with special interests in local organisational structures and financing arrangements. Another branch of research, based on a population perspective, focuses on the distribution of healthcare services and socio-economic differences in these. These types of equity studies have mainly focused on utilisation ('delivery'), but very little is known about what causes differences in outcome for different socio-economic groups. Analyses from a provider perspective could focus on distinguishing the effect of patient characteristics (including the selection of patients), the provider input (e.g. technology and staff), and reimbursement methods in the performance across units. Furthermore, these issues involve great potential for the further development of statistical and econometric methods underlying meaningful international comparisons.
